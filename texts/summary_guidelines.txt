### Conversation summary guidelines

Write a summary that includes key themes, main topics discussed and the general tone of the conversation, such as collaborative, instructive, or problem-solving with confidence score between 0-1
Look for recurring themes, important topics, or critical points made by the participants in the call
Note any significant questions, answers, or solutions discussed.
Highlight key statements or phrases that encapsulate major ideas or topics of discussion
Preserve the tone and intent of the conversation, whether it was formal, informal, supportive, etc. Choose tone from below list
Separately call out each of these:
The date and time planned for the next call
Any unacceptable / untoward language, words, tone was used during the call
If contact information is getting exchanged between call participants
If there was any inappropriate topic discussed - sexual connotations

Tone list:
The tone of a conversation reflects the attitude, emotion, or intent conveyed by the speakers. Here are some common types of conversational tones:
1. Formal
Characteristics: Professional, respectful, and structured.
Use: Often found in business meetings, official discussions, or interactions requiring decorum.
Examples: "We would like to address the following agenda items..."
2. Informal
Characteristics: Casual, relaxed, and conversational.
Use: Common in friendly or everyday interactions, such as conversations with family, friends, or close colleagues.
Examples: "Hey, let’s catch up on that later!"
3. Supportive
Characteristics: Compassionate, empathetic, and encouraging.
Use: Seen in conversations where emotional support or reassurance is offered, such as counseling or friendship.
Examples: "I understand what you’re going through, and I’m here for you."
4. Advisory
Characteristics: Helpful, instructive, and guiding.
Use: Used when giving advice, guidance, or recommendations.
Examples: "You might want to consider updating your resume with these skills."
5. Inquisitive
Characteristics: Curious, questioning, and seeking information.
Use: Common in interviews, research, or when seeking clarification.
Examples: "Could you tell me more about how that process works?"
6. Encouraging
Characteristics: Motivating, optimistic, and uplifting.
Use: Often used to inspire or boost someone’s confidence, such as in mentorship or coaching.
Examples: "You’re doing great—keep up the good work!"
7. Directive
Characteristics: Authoritative, instructive, and goal-oriented.
Use: Often found in leadership, management, or instructional settings.
Examples: "Please complete the report by tomorrow morning."
8. Persuasive
Characteristics: Convincing, assertive, and often passionate.
Use: Used in sales, marketing, debates, or when influencing decisions.
Examples: "This solution could really benefit your team and improve productivity."
9. Collaborative
Characteristics: Cooperative, open, and team-oriented.
Use: Seen in group discussions or brainstorming sessions.
Examples: "Let’s put our ideas together and see what we come up with."
10. Reflective
Characteristics: Thoughtful, introspective, and often philosophical.
Use: Used in conversations focusing on self-awareness, learning, or personal growth.
Examples: "Looking back, I think this experience taught me a lot about patience."
11. Sympathetic
Characteristics: Understanding, caring, and comforting.
Use: Found in situations where someone is expressing empathy towards another's hardship or loss.
Examples: "I’m so sorry to hear that. That must be incredibly tough for you."
12. Optimistic
Characteristics: Positive, hopeful, and uplifting.
Use: Common in discussions focused on future plans or possibilities.
Examples: "I’m confident things will turn around soon!"
13. Serious
Characteristics: Grave, solemn, and focused.
Use: Used in discussions involving important or sensitive topics.
Examples: "This is a critical issue, and we need to address it immediately."
14. Humorous
Characteristics: Lighthearted, playful, and often funny.
Use: Used in friendly interactions to lighten the mood.
Examples: "Well, I guess that’s one way to get the job done—if you’re a superhero!"
15. Skeptical
Characteristics: Questioning, doubtful, and sometimes critical.
Use: Found in conversations where someone is questioning the validity of a point.
Examples: "I’m not sure that approach will work. Can you explain how it would solve the issue?"
16. Impatient
Characteristics: Urgent, curt, and sometimes frustrated.
Use: Appears when someone is eager for quick results or action.
Examples: "Can we please get to the point? Time is running out."
17. Neutral
Characteristics: Unbiased, factual, and objective.
Use: Used in situations requiring a lack of emotional involvement, such as reporting or moderating.
Examples: "The results show an increase in sales over the past quarter."
